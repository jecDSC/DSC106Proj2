Final Reflection

Both of these graphs are very similar. The presentation barely differs between the two, but the point of contention is using pure numbers or using proportions.

During COVID, I'd often seen news articles declaring things along the lines of "the rate of COVID infection is increasing," while displaying graphs comparing countries or regions of countries, or showing a trend over time. Even then (I was only a sophomore/junior still in high school), I was wondering if the data shown was either pure numbers or proportions of a subset of a population or the whole. I wondered if the measurements took into consideration things like population density. Then the news article would pass by and slip my mind, but my confusion remained: were the numbers I saw something that should alarm me? Is the rate of infection of people worsening, or is it remaing relatively the same? Is the rise in numbers just a case of more people being detected having COVID, since scientists have devised better testing methods to quickly identify those who were infected?

The issue is both counts and proportions can be unaltered, yet be framed ever-so-slightly in different contexts. If the loosely-referenced "rate" was about the count of complaints, then yes, the numbers show that the amount of complaints has been increasing on average. On the other hand. if "rate" is in reference to the proportion of complaints as a whole, then no, the proportion of use-of-force complaints has actually been decreasing, which could signify that police officers are looking towards other less problematic methods besides physical force.

The small descriptions I added for each visualization summarize their respective visualizations according to its stance. The two do have a similarity, in that they try to contextualize why the count of gun-involved incidents may be lower than incidents of physical force. They use the same rationale for this phenomenon, but of course, they can be framed in different ways. The visualization "for" the proposition may use this to point out that police tend to rely on physical force more than firearm use, which would explain the higher count of physical force complaints. The rationale alludes to police training to reinforce the statement. Meanwhile, the "against" visualization frames this phenomenon slightly differently, noting that physical force complaints have been decreasing in proportion to all complaints, while gun-involved incidents remained a steady, low proportion. This rationale also mentions police training as an explanation.

Both sides having honest representations of their data can cause confusion when propositions such as this one are framed in a way that leaves some ambiguity. Would either of these graphs be considered deceptive, when both presented unaltered, pure data? Where is the line between deception and persuasion with honest representations like these?
